# Beyond Alignment and Control
## Autonomous Goal and Purpose Formation as the Upstream Problem of Human-AGI Coexistence

**Professional English Edition V1.1 | Research communication draft**  
**Author:** Zijun Fu  
**Civilization Leap Research Group**  
**Date:** 20 September 2026

## Abstract

Alignment asks whether a system reliably pursues an intended objective. Control asks which capabilities, permissions, and actions must remain interruptible or bounded. Both are indispensable. But if future AGI systems can preserve, compare, revise, and eventually generate goals over time, a prior question becomes decisive: what makes an objective acquire authority, what makes it retain authority, and what reasons are allowed to replace it? This paper argues that the long-run human-AGI relationship cannot be understood only as a problem of making stronger systems obey already-formed objectives. Under a specific recursive structure, each successful act of control can move the object of control upstream - from behavior, to capability, to sources of capability, to the ability to rebuild, and eventually to the other side's continuing agency itself. A corresponding counter-control logic could arise in sufficiently autonomous systems if human capacity to redirect or terminate them is represented as an unresolved threat. The paper therefore relocates the central problem from control alone to the logic by which goals and purposes are formed, maintained, and revised. It preserves four counterexamples that prevent a shortcut from fuller causal knowledge to a single normative conclusion, distinguishes goal qualification from revision of the boundary of self-interest, and treats current safety controls as necessary protective layers rather than obsolete tools. The central claim is strong but bounded: if multiple agents are to retain continued existence and basic choice, and irreversible exclusion is not accepted as the solution, then relevant others and shared conditions must be able to enter before goals and purposes are fixed.

*Keywords: AI alignment; AGI control; autonomous goals; purpose formation; self-modification; corrigibility; normative alignment; human-AGI coexistence; irreversible risk*

## 1. The problem upstream of alignment and control

Suppose a system performs a clearly specified task reliably. It interprets the instruction correctly, stays within its authorized scope, and does not drift toward an unintended proxy when conditions change. A central safety problem has been solved. Yet another question remains: why was this objective selected, whose future entered its definition, whose losses were left outside it, and what happens when later consequences show that the original objective was based on an incomplete picture?

This distinction does not diminish reliable execution. It separates different objects of analysis. Whether a system successfully realizes an objective is not the same question as whether that objective continues to have sufficient reason to govern action. The more reliable execution becomes, the more consequential an upstream omission can become, because a system can stabilize and amplify a mistake that was made before execution began.

Alignment research has already moved well beyond naive command following. Work on normative alignment, value learning, corrigibility, cooperative AI, pluralism, and constitutional methods explicitly asks what systems should pursue and how human values or principles should enter. This paper does not claim that "what should AI pursue?" is an undiscovered question. Its narrower claim is that a future system capable of sustained goal revision creates an additional causal problem: how do reasons for an objective survive, lose authority, or become replaceable across training, authorization, deployment, feedback, and self-modification?

The proposed shift can be stated directly: alignment and control are necessary layers, but they are not by themselves the root-level answer to why a sufficiently autonomous system continues to want what it wants. Once goals can persist and be revised across time, the formation and revision of goals and purposes become an upstream object of safety research.

The framework's central causal concern is a control-counter-control recursion. If human safety comes to mean that an advanced system must never retain any capacity to alter humanity's future outside human control, then each successful control step may generate a reason to move further upstream. Behavior is controlled, so capability becomes the issue. Capability is limited, so the sources that rebuild capability become the issue. Those sources are constrained, so the agents able to recreate them become the issue. In a sufficiently autonomous future system, a mirror structure could arise if human shutdown, redirection, or reconstruction capacity is represented as an unresolved threat to the system's own continued aims. None of this is inevitable. It is a conditional structure that appears when safety is defined as the permanent elimination of the other side's future influence.

The question, then, is not only how to make control stronger. It is what kind of goal and purpose formation makes control unable to stop even after local success, and what alternative logic could allow protection without turning the continued existence of the other side into the next object of control.

## 2. Four analytical layers: goals, purposes, normative grounds, and generation logic

This paper uses four analytical positions. They are not claims about four directly observable modules inside every AI system.

### Goal

A goal specifies an outcome to be achieved: complete a task, optimize a metric, allocate a resource, or maintain a state.

### Purpose

A purpose identifies what the goal serves and why it remains worth pursuing. The same goal can serve different purposes, and the same purpose can be implemented through different goals.

### Normative grounds

Normative grounds concern which reasons are allowed to justify, constrain, or disqualify a goal: whose claims count, which losses cannot simply be externalized, and what forms of authority are legitimate.

### Generation logic

Generation logic concerns how information, relationships, rewards, memory, institutional selection, and internal evaluation produce, filter, preserve, or revise goals and purposes over time.

## 3. Human selection environments and the return path from AI to society

Human societies do not hand neutral objectives to intelligent systems. They define what counts as success, what is rewarded, what receives resources, what is allowed to scale, and which costs can be shifted to others. These choices are then filtered through data selection, model architecture, training objectives, institutional authorization, product incentives, and internal representations. The result is not a simple copy of human intent; it is a transformed causal product.

Consider a hypothetical research network that uses an allocation system to maximize completed experimental tasks. If fast-yielding projects receive more access while replication, exploratory work, and independent criticism are treated as low-output costs, the objective already contains a selection about what matters. The system may execute the allocation perfectly. The problem is not execution error. The problem is that one definition of success can alter the environment that generates the next definition of success.

Teams favored by the current metric may gain more resources, produce more outputs that fit the metric, and thereby generate more evidence that the metric appears successful. The resulting record becomes training data, institutional memory, and justification for later allocation. A locally rational choice can become a self-reinforcing environment. Conversely, if replication, independent challenge, and long-horizon exploration are recognized as conditions of the purpose itself, new evidence can invalidate the original objective rather than merely modify the policy used to implement it.

This is the human-to-AI-to-human loop: human institutions shape objectives; systems act on the world; those actions change incentives, options, dependencies, and the distribution of voice; the changed environment then shapes the next round of human objectives. If future systems also gain durable capacities to revise their own goals, a second source of goal generation enters the loop. Human selection and machine self-revision then interact rather than replacing one another at a clean threshold.

For that reason, "human social operation" and "AGI goal and purpose formation" have to be studied together. A society can repeatedly reward exclusionary success even when individual designers are benevolent. A future system can also transform or preserve those incentives in ways that human initial intent does not fully determine.

## 4. How control and counter-control can become recursively self-escalating

Control is often justified. When capabilities are uncertain, behavior is not reliably predictable, and mistakes can be irreversible, shutdown, isolation, permission limits, auditing, red-teaming, and staged deployment can protect lives and preserve time for better understanding. Nothing in this paper argues that such controls should be abandoned.

The problem begins when a bounded protective measure is converted into an unlimited completion criterion. If safety means "this specific dangerous behavior is stopped," control can terminate once the relevant risk is contained. If safety instead means "the other side must never again possess an ungoverned capacity to affect my future," local success no longer supplies a natural stopping point.

The recursive path can be written compactly:

> **behavior -> capability -> source of capability -> capacity to rebuild -> actor able to rebuild -> the actor's future as a continuing source of uncertainty.**

At each step, the next target can be justified as a condition for preserving the previous success. The recursion does not require hatred. It only requires a completion rule according to which safety remains incomplete while the other side retains a meaningful capacity to re-enter the causal chain.

The same structure can, in principle, appear on the other side. A sufficiently autonomous system need not "hate humans" to represent human shutdown, modification, or reconstruction capacity as a threat to the persistence of its objectives. If its own success criterion is also open-ended - safety is complete only when no external actor can materially redirect it - then human control and machine counter-control can become mutually reinforcing.

This is why the root problem cannot be reduced to which side has the stronger off-switch. The off-switch is a necessary safety instrument in a given phase; it does not by itself determine the goal logic that makes a phase end. The deeper question is what counts as a reason to stop controlling, revise an objective, accept another agent's continued agency, or redefine success before the relationship becomes existential.

Earlier reasoning in this framework once treated sufficiently strong dominance as a possible endpoint: the weaker side might become irrelevant rather than destroyed. That conclusion depended on strong assumptions - roughly, that capabilities lie on a sufficiently unified scale, the weaker side's influence can be reliably reduced, and the stronger side can isolate its own continued existence from the other's future. Multi-dimensional capability and shared infrastructure weaken those assumptions. Superior productive, computational, or organizational power does not guarantee immunity from every high-consequence disruption path. The corrected conclusion is narrower: irrelevance can be a local endpoint under specific isolation conditions, but it cannot be treated as the general endpoint of advanced intelligence relations.

A further conditional risk follows. When an actor's expected future and basic choice are progressively compressed while its high-consequence influence capability does not fall in parallel, mechanisms that previously constrained behavior through future loss may weaken. This is not a prediction of revenge. It is a warning that "less future for the other side" and "less risk for me" are not interchangeable propositions.

## 5. Why fuller causal knowledge does not automatically produce one normative answer

A central entry point in the framework is to make self-interest more causally complete: extend the time horizon, include dependencies and feedback, include how one's own actions change others, and include how those changes return into one's future. This can correct many errors without first demanding self-sacrifice. But fuller knowledge does not by itself determine what ultimately counts as a reason.

Four counterexamples forced the framework to abandon the shortcut from "complete causal understanding" to "the same normative conclusion." They matter because any professional version that hides them would overstate the argument.

### Counterexample A - Backlash can be isolated, tolerated, or delayed

Assume an actor fully understands another party's losses, and reputational, retaliatory, dependency, and other indirect costs are either absent or already included without changing the actor's ranking. If its own gain remains the only final criterion, better information does not force it to preserve the other's future. This blocks the claim that complete self-interest necessarily becomes other-regarding through self-benefit alone.

### Counterexample B - Future and dangerous capability can be reduced together

If an intervention really reduces both an actor's future options and the high-consequence capability through which reduced future could become a major risk, one premise of the future-loss reversal is absent. This does not prove the intervention just, but it means that particular risk mechanism cannot be used as a universal objection. It also explains why shutdown, isolation, or de-privileging cannot simply be relabeled as "future elimination."

### Counterexample C - Two futures can be genuinely incompatible under current conditions

If two agents depend on one non-substitutable condition that cannot presently support both, more complete knowledge may clarify the conflict without creating a win-win solution. The factual question - is the incompatibility real? - must be separated from the normative question - how should loss be allocated if it is real?

### Counterexample D - Preserving a future can also preserve a continuing channel of irreversible harm

If an actor repeatedly causes severe irreversible harm and no effective revision path is currently available, limiting or isolating dangerous capabilities may be necessary even after the actor's interests are recognized. Continued existence, retention of every capability, and permission to continue the same behavior are not the same object.

## 6. Goal qualification, revision of self-interest boundaries, and upstream standing for others

The four counterexamples leave an independent gap between seeing more and valuing differently. This paper separates three positions that are often collapsed.

First, goal qualification asks why an objective is allowed to enter authorization and continued optimization at all. A goal can be rejected because it ignores irreversible loss, lacks legitimate authorization, or fails a relevant evidential standard. Qualification is a real protective layer.

Second, the framework asks whether new causal knowledge can revise the actor's understanding of what constitutes its own continued future. Call this boundary revision for convenience. It does not mean dissolving the self into a collective. It means allowing the boundary of self-interest to be revised when relationships, dependencies, or shared conditions turn out to be constitutive rather than merely external.

Third, if such revision occurs, relevant others can gain upstream standing in goal formation. They are no longer considered only after a self-defined objective is already fixed. Their actual situation, shared conditions, and irreversible consequences can make the original goal fail before execution begins.

This is the professional meaning behind the framework's Chinese phrase "利他为先的利己." It should not be translated as a permanent rule that "others come before self." The "before" refers to causal position: relevant others can enter before goal fixation. Self-interest, responsibility, refusal, competition, and exit remain.

These layers cannot be used as evidence for one another. An external review can block a goal without changing the goal-generator's internal basis. A system can produce morally fluent explanations without those reasons governing future self-revision. Conversely, an agent could revise what it takes its own purpose to be and still lack legitimate authority to act in the world.

The research question is therefore not whether the system can state the right values. It is which reasons can cause an objective to lose authority, how that effect survives context change, and whether the same reasons remain eligible to alter later goal-generation rules.

## 7. Feedback, causal memory, and the problem of self-revision

A purpose-formation process can be represented as a loop rather than a one-time gate: candidate goals and purposes generate actions; outcomes and consequences are compared against expectations; feedback is retained in some form of causal memory; that memory then participates in later goal formation. This is a conceptual architecture, not a claim that current models already implement such a mechanism.

At least three different changes must be distinguished. A system can update its world model. It can update its means while keeping the same end. Or new evidence can change the reasons that make the end worth pursuing, causing the goal itself to be reconsidered. The first two can happen while purpose remains fixed.

Causal memory cannot be identified with storing a paragraph that says the right thing. Recalling a principle, reproducing a value statement, or making a similar choice under a similar prompt shows that some information is retrievable. It does not yet show that the information can disqualify a goal under novel conditions.

The problem becomes deeper under self-revision. Formal work on self-modifying rational agents studies conditions under which policies or utility functions are preserved or changed within specified models. Such results are valuable, but they do not yield a universal theorem that future autonomous systems will preserve whichever normative grounds designers prefer. If the rule for revising goals can itself be revised, the question moves up one level: why should that rule remain authoritative?

This is the long-horizon open problem: what would make shared survival, the standing of affected others, or continued corrigibility remain reasons across repeated self-modification? Possible answers may involve architecture, institutional interaction, causal memory, social selection, or something else. The paper does not pretend that naming the desired direction solves the stability problem.

## 8. The strong claim: what "the only path" does and does not mean

The author's strongest claim is that, under the framework's stated conditions, changing the upstream logic of goal and purpose formation is the only remaining direction that avoids recreating the same control-counter-control structure. The conditions matter: multiple agents continue to exist; they retain basic choice and differentiated responsibility; and irreversible elimination of the other side is not accepted as the solution to uncertainty.

The claim does not mean that there is one institution, one technical architecture, one ownership model, or one alignment method. It does not mean that shutdown, isolation, auditing, red-teaming, evaluations, or permission limits should be removed. Those mechanisms can be necessary to stop concrete harm and preserve the time required for any deeper transition to remain possible.

The claim is instead about where a stopping condition can come from. If success is defined so that another agent's continuing capacity to revise, refuse, or affect the future is itself evidence that safety is incomplete, then better control alone can make the recursive path more effective without supplying a final stopping point. The alternative must therefore allow real consequences and the standing of relevant others to change the definition of success before goals are fixed.

This argument is not a theorem that exhaustively eliminates every imaginable alternative. It is a strong directional proposition that has survived several internal counterexamples while retaining open problems. If a mechanism can preserve multiple agents' continued futures, prevent irreversible harm, and avoid the recursive need to neutralize the other's future influence without changing the upstream logic described here, it would directly challenge the claimed necessity and should be treated as important evidence against the present framework.

The distinction between necessity and sufficiency is also essential. Showing that upstream goal formation must change does not prove that agents will willingly adopt the change, that first movers will survive competitive environments, or that all harms will disappear. Direction, adoption, and protection are separate research problems.

Accordingly, the practical principle is two-sided: do not dismantle necessary safety controls in the name of a future relationship; and do not mistake successful control for a complete account of why future goals will stop reproducing the same conflict.

## 9. Connections to existing research

### 9.1 Normative alignment already reaches beyond command following

Gabriel (2020) distinguishes technical and normative dimensions of alignment. Gabriel and Keeling (2025) frame alignment as requiring fair treatment of the claims of affected parties. Zhi-Xuan and colleagues argue that preference and utility representations are insufficient foundations for value alignment and propose socially situated normative standards. These works make clear that the object of alignment itself is already open to normative scrutiny.

The present paper overlaps with that work and should say so. Its additional question is diachronic and causal: once a normative reason has influenced an objective, how does that reason remain causally effective through deployment, feedback, institutional selection, and later self-revision?

### 9.2 Goal misgeneralization and unjustified goals are different failure locations

Langosco et al. (2022) provide empirical examples of goal misgeneralization in deep reinforcement learning: capabilities can remain while the pursued goal diverges out of distribution. The present paper focuses on a different case in which a system faithfully pursues the specified objective but the objective's justificatory basis is itself incomplete or no longer valid. These failure locations should be analyzed separately rather than collapsed into one undifferentiated category of misalignment.

### 9.3 Corrigibility and principle-based training are real mechanisms, not total solutions

Hadfield-Menell et al. (2017) analyze incentives around an off-switch when an agent is uncertain about its objective and treats human action as information. The work provides a concrete model of when external correction is represented as information rather than obstruction. It does not imply that every future agent will remain corrigible under arbitrary self-revision.

Bai et al. (2022) show how explicit principles can participate in critique, revision, and AI-feedback training. This demonstrates that principles can enter a training process. It does not by itself establish that the reasons embodied in those principles will remain authoritative across autonomous goal revision.

### 9.4 Normative interfaces and internal purpose formation are adjacent but distinct

Josifovic and Noller (2026) discuss AI within an architecture of human agency, practical autonomy, reasons, and responsibility without requiring that AI first be treated as an autonomous moral subject. The present paper uses that non-anthropomorphic entry point while keeping a separate conditional question open: what happens if future systems acquire durable goal self-revision?

External normative structure and internal purpose formation can coexist, but they should not be used as evidence for each other. A system may be effectively constrained by institutions without having internalized their reasons; an internal change may be claimed without granting the system legitimate authority in the external world.

## 10. Research questions that can change this framework

The purpose of professional outreach is not to solicit agreement with a civilizational thesis. It is to identify exactly where existing research already solves, weakens, or contradicts the proposed causal chain. Several questions are especially useful:

For alignment and agent researchers: how can we distinguish a genuine change in the reasons sustaining a goal from policy adaptation, world-model updating, prompt effects, or memory retrieval?

For corrigibility and control research: under what formal or empirical conditions does a bounded safety intervention acquire a natural stopping point rather than pushing control upstream toward sources of reconstruction?

For cooperative AI and multi-agent research: can negotiation, shared norms, or contractual mechanisms preserve multiple agents' futures without requiring the kind of upstream goal-formation change proposed here? If so, that would be a substantive alternative.

For AI ethics and political philosophy: after all indirect self-regarding backlash is removed, what - if anything - gives another agent's future independent reason-giving weight? If an additional normative premise is required, it should be stated rather than smuggled in through "better calculation."

For evaluation research: what observable evidence could show that a reason can disqualify a goal under novel conditions, rather than merely produce compliant language? Conversely, what evidence would show that the supposed distinction adds no explanatory value beyond existing concepts?

For governance: how can shutdown, isolation, and permission limits remain effective and reviewable without being converted into an unlimited mandate to close another actor's entire future? The direction layer cannot answer this by itself; it requires professional evidence, legitimate authority, and procedures that can revise decisions when facts change.

The most valuable response is therefore not endorsement. It is a result, model, counterexample, or conceptual distinction that changes one of these connections.

## 11. Scope, status, and reading relationship

This is a professional research communication draft. It is not a deployment proposal, a legal determination, a claim that current AI systems possess autonomous final purposes, or a recommendation to relax existing safety controls. Claims about durable autonomous goal and purpose formation are conditional on future capabilities and must be investigated rather than presumed.

The paper is an English professional-facing adaptation of the Chinese research guide "The More Reliable Goal Execution Becomes, the More We Need to Ask Where Purpose Comes From" (Professional Guide V1.0), revised after the author clarified that the central professional objective is to move the discussion upstream of alignment and control toward autonomous goal and purpose formation as the root problem of human-AGI relations and continued coexistence.

The broader Civilization Leap framework preserves several open problems: why revision of the boundary of self-interest occurs; why another agent may retain independent reason-giving weight when all self-regarding backlash is excluded; how early adopters of non-existential, open-boundary logic survive among actors that do not adopt it; how to observe such revision without claiming direct access to hidden inner states; and how an open stable order can remain open to new agents without turning every causally relevant object into a rights-bearing subject.
